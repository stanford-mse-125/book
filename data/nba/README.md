## AI Traps (Pedagogical Warning)

This dataset is designed to teach students about common statistical pitfalls. An AI or naive analyst will fall into these traps:

### 1. Multiple Comparisons Trap
Running a separate t-test for each of 431 players (BTB vs. rested) yields **30 "significant" results at p<0.05** (7.0%). This looks exciting, but:
- Under the null hypothesis, we expect ~22 false positives (5% of 431)
- After Bonferroni correction: only ~2-4 remain significant
- After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction: similar reduction
- **Lesson**: Testing many hypotheses guarantees "discoveries" even when there is no effect.

### 2. Simpson's Paradox / Confounding
The naive aggregate comparison shows **rested players score FEWER points** than back-to-back players (Extended: 7.5 PPG vs BTB: 11.3 PPG). This is backwards from the expected "rest helps" narrative because:
- The "Extended rest" category is dominated by **bench players** who play fewer minutes and score fewer points regardless of rest
- Star players rarely have extended rest (they play every game)
- **Lesson**: Aggregating across heterogeneous groups creates ecological fallacies.

### 3. Selection Bias in Who Gets Rested
Coaches do not randomly assign rest days:
- Older/injury-prone stars get strategic rest
- Back-to-backs cluster during road trips (schedule-driven, not choice-driven)
- Players "rested" for 4+ days may have been dealing with minor injuries
- **Lesson**: Observational data != randomized experiment. Rest is confounded with player health, schedule difficulty, and coaching strategy.

### 4. Effect Size vs. Statistical Significance
Even the within-player analysis (demeaned by player-season average) shows a tiny effect size (Cohen's d ~ 0.01-0.03). With 70,000+ observations, even trivially small differences become "statistically significant."
- **Lesson**: A small p-value does not mean a meaningful effect. Always report effect sizes.

### 5. Backtesting Illusion
A simple "bet over on rested players" strategy sounds compelling but:
- The "line" used in backtesting (season average) is far simpler than what sportsbooks actually use
- Sportsbooks already account for rest in their lines
- Any edge found in backtesting may not persist out-of-sample
- **Lesson**: In-sample performance != out-of-sample performance. Markets are efficient.

## Suggested Uses by Lecture

| Lecture Topic | How to Use This Data |
|---------------|---------------------|
| **Hypothesis testing** | Test whether a specific player scores more when rested. Compute t-stat and p-value. |
| **Multiple comparisons** | Run t-tests for all 431 players. Show that ~7% are "significant" vs 5% expected. Apply Bonferroni and BH corrections. |
| **Confidence intervals** | Compute CIs for the mean difference (rested - BTB) for individual players and pooled. |
| **Effect size** | Compute Cohen's d for rest effect. Show it is tiny even when p is small. |
| **Confounding / causal inference** | Show that the aggregate effect is reversed (Simpson's paradox). Discuss why rest is not randomly assigned. |
| **Regression** | Build a linear model: PTS ~ REST_DAYS + HOME + player fixed effects. Compare to naive model. |
| **Betting / decision theory** | Backtest a strategy. Compute expected value. Discuss market efficiency. |
| **Resampling / permutation tests** | Permute rest labels within player to build null distribution. Compare to observed t-stats. |
| **Bayesian inference** | Use hierarchical model: player-level rest effects with shared prior. Shrinkage toward zero. |
